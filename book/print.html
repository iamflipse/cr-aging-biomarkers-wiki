Caloric Restriction & Biological Aging

Caloric restriction (CR) — a sustained reduction in energy intake below ad libitum, without malnutrition — is the best-studied non-pharmacological intervention for extending lifespan in model organisms, where it consistently extends lifespan and delays age-related disease onset across 10–40% restriction levels.

Human evidence

  • CALERIE (Comprehensive Assessment of Long-term Effects of Reducing Intake of Energy): 2-year trial of a mean 11.9% achieved CR in young/middle-aged normal-weight adults; showed significant improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors.
  • CALERIE epigenetic-clock sub-study: 2 years of 25% CR slowed the pace of aging as measured by DunedinPACE, but did not produce detectable change in static epigenetic-age measures — measures tracking ongoing change over time appear more sensitive to short interventions than measures estimating a fixed "biological age."
  • SELECT trial (secondary analysis): cardiovascular benefit (~30% MACE reduction) was driven largely by reduction in waist circumference rather than total weight loss — cardiovascular benefit may depend on weight-independent mechanisms.

Animal models

  • Lifespan extension with ~40% CR appears independent of body fat levels; in some studies the longest-lived animals within CR groups have the highest fat mass.
  • In genetically diverse outbred mice, mice that maintain relatively greater body weight/fat mass under CR show the greatest longevity.

The Composite Biomarker Index

The study's core instrument: a composite index of 6 blood-based biomarkers selected via the TAME (Targeting Aging with Metformin) Biomarkers Workgroup framework — markers with the strongest evidence for capturing core aging hallmarks (inflammation, insulin/IGF-1 signaling, cellular stress) that remain feasible in large geroscience trials.

How the index is scored

Each biomarker's change (follow-up minus baseline) is assigned to a quintile, and the quintiles are summed — the "quintile sum score." Improvements in the index = a lower sum (markers moving in the favorable direction).

Why a composite

  • Single biomarkers like GDF-15 and cystatin C showed considerable between-study heterogeneity; the composite index gave a consistent signal across all 7 pooled trials.
  • Composite indices reduce the variability inherent to single-biomarker approaches and reflect real-time physiological response rather than a fixed "biological age" — unlike epigenetic clocks or telomere length, which correlate only weakly-to-moderately with each other (each reflects a different aspect of aging).

Prior validation

The same panel was previously validated in a randomized CR + exercise trial in older adults with HFpEF, where it demonstrated sensitivity to intervention-related change in a comparably aged, comorbid population (ref 14).

CR Effects on the Index

Composite index (quintile sum score)

  • CR → index: −2.2 (95% CI −2.9 to −1.5) — CR was consistently associated with index improvement; study-specific estimates were all < 0 (Supp. Fig 1a).
  • After adjusting for weight change (model 3): the CR effect attenuated to −1.2 (95% CI −2.0 to −0.3) — the residual direct (weight-independent) effect (Supp. Fig 1c).
  • Imputed and non-imputed analyses gave consistent conclusions.

Weight-Loss Effects on the Index

Weight change as predictor of the index (model 2)

  • Each 1 kg of weight loss was associated with a +0.22 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.34) increase in the quintile sum score of change — study-specific estimates all > 0 (Supp. Fig 1b).
  • Larger weight decreases → larger improvements in the index, particularly for CRP, IL-6, insulin, TNF-R1; GDF-15 and CysC were heterogeneous.

Weight-Independent Mechanisms

The persistent CR effect after adjusting for weight change implies weight-independent biological pathways. The paper discusses several candidate mechanisms (inferential in this analysis, not directly measured):

  • AMPK activation + SIRT1 upregulation + mTOR suppression: nutrient-sensing pathways; mTOR suppression stimulates autophagy of damaged proteins and organelles.
  • Oxidative stress + chronic low-grade inflammation: the same AMPK/SIRT1/mTOR mechanisms are linked to reducing oxidative stress and attenuating chronic inflammation — a plausible bridge to the observed CRP and IL-6 improvements independent of weight loss.
  • Mitochondrial adaptations & immunometabolic shifts in innate immune cell function — implicated in CR benefits in animal models; possible pathway to the insulin-sensitivity improvements observed.

Animal-model support:

  • Lifespan extension with ~40% CR is independent of body fat: in some studies the longest-lived CR animals had the highest fat mass; in outbred mice, those maintaining greater weight/fat under CR showed greatest longevity.
  • Consistent with CALERIE/SELECT evidence of metabolic and inflammatory benefits beyond weight loss (SELECT: ~30% MACE reduction driven largely by waist-circumference change, not total weight loss).

Limitations & Future Directions

Limitations

  1. Single research network: all 7 trials came from the Wake Forest IASDR — generalizability to broader populations is limited.
  2. Short durations: intervention durations ranged from 6 to 18 months (IM-FIT 5 mo, MEDIFAST 6 mo, INFINITE 20 wk, HALLO-P 9 mo, CLIP/EMPOWER/IDEA 18 mo); findings are best read as evidence that CR favorably modified inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers, not yet as proof of long-term disease/mortality benefit.
  3. Heterogeneity: GDF-15 and TNF-R1 study-specific estimates were variable; MEDIFAST (n=31) was underpowered (mediation model did not converge).
  4. Exercise confounding: exercise modality could not be fully adjusted for (confounded with study); sensitivity analysis restricted to CLIP/EMPOWER/IDEA (the only trials with exercise variation) left primary findings unchanged.
  5. Composite vs. other aging measures: correlation between this index and epigenetic clocks (DunedinPACE, static epigenetic age) or frailty indices was not addressed.
  6. Inferential mechanisms: AMPK/SIRT1/mTOR/autophagy pathways are proposed, not directly measured in this analysis.

Future directions

  • Validate the composite index in independent, more diverse populations.
  • Establish its relationship to hard endpoints: mortality, disability, functional decline.
  • Correlate the index with epigenetic clocks and validated frailty indices to position it within the geroscience biomarker landscape.
  • Apply the index to large epidemiologic cohorts and pragmatic trials as a surrogate endpoint for aging interventions.
  • Longer follow-up to test whether index improvement predicts slower pace of aging.

Keywords Biomarkers · Aging · Caloric restriction · Older adults Introduction As the population ages, the prevention of age-related diseases and preservation of functional independence have become a major public health priority. There is growing recognition of the need for biomarkers that would reliably indicate how interventions affect the aging process [1, 2]. Reliable biomarkers of aging could be critical tools for evaluating biological aging, monitoring morbidity, and assessing the efficacy of strategies designed to extend healthspan [3]. A key aspect of biomarker evaluation involves the responsiveness of the biomarker to an intervention that is known to affect the aging process. One such intervention is caloric restriction (CR), a sustained reduction in energy below ad libitum intake without malnutrition. In animal models, CR has consistently been shown to extend lifespan and delay the onset of age-related diseases [4, 5]. Animal models have also Vol.: (0123456789)

In human, the CALERIE (Comprehensive Assessment of Long-term Effects of Reducing Intake of Energy) trial demonstrated that a 2-year, mean achieved CR of 11.9% intervention in young- and middle-aged, normal-weight adults had significant improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors, suggesting potential translational benefit [7, 8]. The CALERIE intervention affected biomarkers of aging including a measure of biologic age based on a set of age-related clinical chemistries, and one methylomic measure associated with the pace of aging [9, 10]. In addition, a meta-analysis of randomized trials including a CR arm showed a 15% reduction in all-cause mortality [11]. Recent findings from large study of GLP-1 receptor agonists also suggest benefit for all-cause mortality [12]. Justice et al. [13] proposed criteria for selecting biomarkers for evaluation of therapies intended to affect the aging process that would satisfy many conditions necessary to qualify the biomarkers as potential surrogate biomarkers. The criteria included (1) measurement reliability and feasibility; (2) relevance to aging; (3) robust and consistent ability to predict all-cause mortality, clinical and functional outcomes independent of age; and (4) responsiveness to intervention [13]. Based on a survey of the literature, they identified nine biomarkers and recommended the construction of a composite index rather than considering each biomarker individually as a way to dampen interindividual variability in intervention response. This index was shown to change in response to CR but not exercise in a randomized trial of 100 older persons with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [14]. It is not known whether this index applies more generally, and it is also unclear whether there is a dose-response relationship between the intensity of CR and the change in the index. To address these questions, we used Wake Forest’s Integrated Aging Studies Databank and Repository (IASDR) which houses data and biospecimens from over 17 completed CR-based intervention trials in older adults. The trials vary in their inclusion criteria, their target weight loss, and the availability of non-CR groups for comparison. Methods Study design and data sources This study is a mediation analysis of completed CR intervention trials in older adults using biospecimens from the Wake Forest Integrated Aging Studies Databank and Repository (IASDR). This present analysis pooled individual data from seven randomized trials to evaluate randomization to CR and changes in weight with biomarkers of aging across varying degrees of CR. Selection of biomarker index participant samples In total, 2126 samples from 1197 unique study participants were considered. Of these, 1176 samples were matched, and 1154 had non-missing treatment group information, representing 1063 unique individuals. Samples were distributed evenly and systematically across 42 plates. There were no significant differences in BMI, age, or sex across plates, confirming that the sample allocation was balanced. All samples with available biomarker measurements (CRP, GDF15, IL-6, insulin, CysC, or TNF-R1) were included, totaling 455 samples—comprising 21 single samples and 217 paired samples. For the remaining 679 samples without biomarker data, 83 single samples were selected to ensure at least one single sample per plate, with 41 plates containing two singles. The remaining 596 slots were filled using pairs from 298 participants (see Supplemental Tables 3 and 4). Description of contribution studies All seven trials enrolled older adults and included at least one intervention arm designed to induce CR, with longitudinal assessments of body weight, physical function, and circulating biomarkers. Across trials, CR interventions were delivered via dietary counseling, structured meal plans, or partial meal

IDEA [Intensive Diet and Exercise for Arthritis] (2006–2010): Included 254 older adults, in which 244 were selected for this study, aged 55 years or older with overweight or obesity (BMI 27–41 kg/ m2) and radiographically confirmed knee osteoarthritis. Caloric restriction exposure CR was defined as randomization to an intervention explicitly designed to reduce daily energy intake relative to baseline requirements. For pooled analysis, CR was treated as a binary exposure (CR versus no CR), while change in body weight was evaluated as a downstream consequence of CR. Biomarker index assessment Biomarkers were selected based on the Targeting Aging with Metformin (TAME) framework [13, 14] and included markers of inflammation, metabolism, and renal function (CRP, IL-6, CysC, insulin, GDF-15, and TNF-R1). The number of samples from a study that was allocated to a plate was done proportionally to the total number of samples in each study, ensuring balanced representation across plates and minimizing confounding between study membership and batch assignment. All samples from the same person (repeated measurements) were included on the same plate to avoid withinperson batch effects, and samples from mixed studies and treatment groups were distributed across plates using a stratified random allocation strategy to maintain balance. Within plates, samples were randomly assigned to positions to reduce potential positional effects. Batch effects were accounted for in downstream analysis by modeling plate as a random effect in the mixed-effects models. Statistical methods Descriptive statistics (means (SDs) for normally distributed variables, medians (interquartile ranges) for non-normally distributed variables, and counts (proportions) for categorical variables) (Table 1) were used to summarize demographics, body mass, physical performance, and biomarker information for each study and overall. Within CR groups, changes in weight and biomarkers (Table 2) were calculated by subtracting post-randomization measurements obtained at the 6-month visit from prerandomization measurements, with means (SDs) used as measures of central tendency within study and overall. groups and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Prior to modeling, we used fully conditional specification multivariate imputation to create 100 separate datasets, using the 14 variables in the imputation procedure (weight and each of the 6 biomarkers at the baseline and follow-up time points). Mixed-effects models, accounting for a random batch effect, were employed to explore the relationships between change in each separate biomarker, in addition to the quintile sum score (i.e., assigning each biomarker’s change to a quintile and summing the quintiles across six biomarkers), and change in weight using the nonimputed dataset and within each imputed dataset. Log transformations were required to allow for the residuals of models analyzing CRP and IL-6 to approximate normality, making change in these variables the log of relative differences. Analyses were performed for each study and overall. We fit a series of models predicting change in each biomarker: (1) model 1: an indicator variable representing randomization to CR versus no CR as the only predictor, (2) model 2: change in weight as the only predictor, and (3) model 3: both the change in weight and the CR indicator variable as predictors. For analysis using the overall dataset, a study effect was additionally included as a fixed effect in each model to account for systematic differences and potential between-study heterogeneity across the seven contributing trials. Results from each of the 100 imputed datasets were summarized by combining the results from the different imputed datasets using the individual estimates and standard errors to derive valid inference. Mediation analysis was conducted to assess whether the CR effect on the outcome measures (i.e., change in each biomarker and the quintile sum score) was mediated through change in weight. Because CR was randomly assigned, confounding of the treatment–mediator and treatment–outcome relationships was minimized by design. Analyses were performed for each study and overall using both non-imputed and imputed datasets. In addition to the above models, we also fit a model that related the CR indicator variable to the mediator (change in weight) as an outcome (model 4). Discussion In this pooled analysis of CR trials in older adults, we demonstrate that CR is consistently associated with improvements in a composite biomarker index reflecting inflammatory and metabolic domains of biological aging. These findings support the utility of

GeroScience

a biomarker index as a sensitive measure of response to geroscience-informed interventions and provide insight into the mechanisms through which CR may influence aging biology. Consistent with prior trials and meta-analyses, CR and weight loss interventions have been shown to improve inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers across populations, supporting their relevance to aging-related pathways [27–32]. The six biomarkers comprising the index (CRP, IL-6, TNF-R1, insulin, GDF-15, and cystatin C) were selected based on the framework proposed by the TAME (Targeting Aging with Metformin) Biomarkers Workgroup, which identified blood-based biomarkers with the strongest evidence for capturing core hallmarks of aging, such as inflammation, insulin/IGF-1 signaling, and cellular stress response, while remaining feasible for measurement in largescale geroscience clinical trials [13]. This panel, and the resulting composite index, has previously been evaluated in a randomized trial of CR and exercise in older adults with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, where it demonstrated sensitivity to intervention-related change in a comparably aged, comorbid population [14]. Building on this prior validation work, we applied the same index across the seven trials pooled to test its consistency and responsiveness to CR across a broader range of study populations and intervention durations. A central finding of this study is that approximately 48.5% (95% CI 22.6 to 82.2%) of the effect of CR on the biomarker index was mediated by weight loss. The effects of CR assignment decreased from −2.2 in the univariate model to −1.2 (95% CI −2.0 to −0.3) after adjusting for weight change. Similarly, the estimated effects of loss decreased from 0.22 to 0.16 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.23) after adjusting for CR assignment. This decrease was expected because the CR assignment and weight loss were strongly related, such that each variable partially accounted for the same variance in the biomarker index improvement. These findings suggest that improvements in the biomarker index are partially, but not completely, mediated by the amount of weight loss. Both CR assignment and magnitude of weight loss were independently associated with favorable changes in the index. This was particularly evident for CRP, IL-6, and insulin, suggesting that reductions in systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation may be key pathways linking CR to improvements in biological aging.

The persistence of CR effects after adjustment for weight change supports the presence of weight-independent mechanisms, consistent with prior evidence from trials such as CALERIE and SELECT demonstrating metabolic and inflammatory benefits beyond weight loss alone [8, 33–35]. These findings are further supported by animal models, where CR extends lifespan and delays the onset of age-related diseases through mechanisms that are not solely dependent on reductions in body weight or adiposity [5, 6]. Notably, lifespan extension with ~40% CR appears to be independent of body fat levels; in some studies, the longest-lived animals within CR groups are those with the highest fat mass. Similarly, in genetically diverse outbred mice, mice that maintain relatively greater body weight and fat mass under CR conditions tend to exhibit the greatest longevity [6]. Several candidate mechanisms could explain this weight-independent benefit. CR is known to activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and sirtuin (SIRT1) while suppressing mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling, a nutrient-sensing pathway that stimulates autophagy of damaged proteins and organelles [36, 37]. Those same mechanisms (AMPK, SIRT1, and mTOR) have been linked to reducing oxidative stress and attenuating chronic low-grade inflammation, offering a plausible bridge to the CRP and IL-6 improvements observed in our study independent of weight loss [38]. Mitochondrial adaptations and immunometabolic shifts in innate immune cell function have also been implicated in CR-associated benefits in animal models, offering possible pathways that could extend to the insulinsensitivity improvements observed in this study [37, 39]. These example pathways offer a biological explanation for why CR assignment retained an independent effect on the biomarker index after adjustment for weight change; though not directly measured in this study, these mechanisms remain inferential in this analysis. Our results extend prior work by demonstrating that a composite biomarker index provides a robust and consistent signal of intervention response across heterogeneous studies. While individual components such as GDF-15 and cystatin C showed variability, the overall index demonstrated consistent improvement with CR and weight loss across cohorts. Prior work comparing DNA-methylation-based epigenetic clock with other approaches found that biomarker indices, telomere length, and epigenetic clocks are only weakly to moderately correlated with each other, suggesting each measure reflects a somewhat different aspect of aging rather than one common process [40]. Similarly, in the CALERIE trial, 2 years of 25% CR slowed the pace of aging as measured by DunedinPACE but did not produce a detectable change in static epigenetic-age measures [10, 41], suggesting that measures which track ongoing change over time may be more sensitive to relatively short intervention than measures that estimate a fixed “biological age.” Based on our observations, this composite biomarker index may similarly reflect real-time physiological response rather than fixed age estimate, possibly accounting for the signal within these intervention durations. Whether this composite biomarker index correlates with epigenetic clocks or validated frailty indices was not addressed here but represents an opportunity for future work as discussed below. These findings have implications for the design of clinical trials targeting aging and healthspan. The responsiveness of the biomarker index to CR, coupled with its partial mediation by weight loss, suggests that such indices may serve as practical surrogate endpoints that capture both upstream biological processes and downstream clinical risk. The potential influence of exercise modality was considered. However, adjustment was not feasible due to confounding with the clinical site variable. In analyses restricted to CLIP, EMPOWER, and IDEA, the only studies with variation in exercise modalities, the results were consistent with the primary findings (data not shown). From a clinical perspective, obesity represents a growing public health concern as obesity is a major driver of multimorbidity, frailty, and disability [2, 6, 42]. The prevalence of obesity is rising in older adults, with recent NHANES data suggesting that about 40% of US adults aged 65 years and older are living with obesity [43]. Interventions that target obesity-related risks could have profound effects on reducing multimorbidity and improving late-life health outcomes [44, 45]. Evidence from a secondary analysis of SELECT trial demonstrated reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) across all Vol:. (1234567890)

weight categories, with cardiovascular benefit (~30%) driven largely by reduction in waist circumference rather than weight loss itself, accounting for approximately one-third of the observed risk reduction [34]. The SELECT trial provided evidence that cardiovascular benefits may depend not only on weight loss itself but on weight-independent mechanisms. This study has several strengths, including the large, pooled sample from multiple rigorously conducted randomized trials, the use of standardized biomarker data, and the application of advanced analytic approaches including mediation modeling and imputation. Although pooling data from multiple cohorts inevitably introduces some degree of heterogeneity due to variations in participant eligibility, intervention intensity, and follow-up duration, several factors mitigated this risk. Specifically, multiple contributing trials utilized highly similar protocols, shared identical principal investigators, and implemented standardized quality control procedures to ensure uniform intervention delivery. Furthermore, study-specific estimates were generally consistent, and sensitivity analyses accounting for exercise modality yielded similar results, suggesting that the primary findings were highly robust and unlikely to be driven by systematic differences among individual studies. Given that the trials pooled here were of relatively short duration, our findings are best interpreted as evidence that CR favorably modified inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers associated with aging pathways. Establishing whether composite index improvement predicts longerterm reductions in the pace of aging, incident disease or mortality, will require longer follow-up and/or application to a larger longitudinal epidemiologic study. However, limitations should be considered. All studies were conducted within a single research network, which may affect generalizability to broader populations. Future work should focus on validating this biomarker index in independent and more diverse populations and further establish its relationship with hard clinical endpoints, including mortality, disability, and functional decline. Future work should also determine how the index relates to other established biological aging measures, including epigenetic clocks and validated frailty indices, to better position it within the broader landscape of geroscience biomarkers. Application of this index in large epidemiologic cohorts and pragmatic trials will be critical to determine its utility as a surrogate endpoint and to refine its role in guiding geroscience-informed interventions.

This integrated analysis demonstrates that CR influences aging-related biomarkers through both weight-dependent and independent pathways, with approximately half of its effects attributable to residual weight-independent pathways. Composite biomarker indices, such as those used here, capture these responses and may serve as surrogate endpoints for aging interventions [35]. Continued refinement of these indices, their applications in large epidemiologic cohorts, and a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying CR’s effects will be essential for advancing biomarker-driven approaches to extending healthspan in older adults.
